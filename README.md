# Beliefs and Preferences in Community College

Despite high labor market returns and low effective tuition, the majority of community college students never earn a two-year degree and only a fraction transfer to four-year programs. I collect novel survey data on the expected choices of community college students, and on their beliefs about earnings, tuition costs, and dropout risks. Beliefs appear to deviate from rational expectations: respondents are optimistic about dropout risk but pessimistic about four-year tuition costs, with an ambiguous net effect on transfer. An information experiment demonstrates that students are responsive to new information, suggesting the potential for correcting biases. In part to quantify the importance of such corrections, I use the data to estimate a dynamic model of community college student choices. The results show that students' plans are most sensitive to the four-year dropout risk, moderately responsive to earnings beliefs, but largely inelastic with respect to expected tuition costs. An exercise in correcting beliefs shifts individuals away from transfer and towards community college dropout, with improved sorting generating significant positive welfare effects. I use the model to test the plausibility of mistaken beliefs as well as two alternative hypotheses for why students' planned outcomes, which include an overwhelming intention to transfer, may differ from the high rates of dropout and low rates of transfer that characterize the community college landscape. This ex-post accounting exercise suggests that learning about four-year dropout risk may play a meaningful role, but is insufficient to close the gap. I demonstrate that ``academic mismatch,'' where students discover they are a worse match for higher education than they believe at the start, is a plausible mechanism for these two phenomena.
