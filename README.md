# Beliefs and Preferences in Community College

Despite high labor market returns and low effective tuition, the majority of community college students never earn a two-year degree and only a fraction transfer to four-year programs. I collect novel survey data on the expected choices of community college students, and on their beliefs about earnings, tuition costs, and dropout risks. Beliefs appear to deviate from rational expectations: respondents underestimate four-year dropout risks but overestimate four-year tuition costs, to ambiguous net effect. An information experiment demonstrates that students are responsive to new information, suggesting the potential for correcting biases. In part to quantify the importance of such corrections, I use the data to estimate a dynamic model of community college student choices. The results show that students' plans are most sensitive to the four-year dropout risk and largely inelastic with respect to expected tuition costs; as a result, counterfactual policy experiments suggest that policymakers can more effectively encourage transfer by decreasing the riskiness of four-year school rather than through tuition subsidies. An exercise in correcting beliefs shifts individuals away from transfer and generates substantial positive welfare effects. Finally, I use the model to examine why students' planned outcomes may differ from the high rates of dropout and low rates of transfer that characterize the community college landscape. This ex-post accounting exercise suggests that learning about four-year dropout risk may play a meaningful role, but is insufficient to close the gap. I demonstrate that "academic mismatch," where students discover they are a worse match for higher education than they believe at the start, is a plausible mechanism for these phenomena.
